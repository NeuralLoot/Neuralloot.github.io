What's real? Real math vs toy parts, sources

The mechanism is real. A language model reads the text so far and gives every token in its vocabulary a score (a "logit"). Softmax turns scores into probabilities: pi = exp(zi/T) / Σj exp(zj/T). Then one token is drawn at random in proportion to those probabilities, appended, and the loop repeats. This page runs exactly that math in your browser: real softmax with temperature T, and real top-p (nucleus) truncation, the smallest set of top tokens whose probabilities add up to at least p, renormalized before the draw (Holtzman et al. 2019, Eq. 2 and 3). The orb is a weighted random draw from the bars you see.

The probabilities are toy. No model ran here. Each prompt has 8 to 12 hand-written candidates with illustrative probabilities (we treat them as the T = 1 distribution, so logits are their logs). Real models score their whole vocabulary: GPT-2's has 50,257 tokens. Tokens are often word pieces, not whole words. "Tomas Ferrel" is a made-up person.

Level 1, picking. Picking the single top word every time is greedy decoding; it is what temperature 0 approaches. Holtzman et al. found greedy text from GPT-2 Large repeated itself in 73.66% of generations (human text: 0.28%), one reason chat apps sample instead.

Level 2, temperature. Dividing scores by T below 1 sharpens the odds toward the favorite; above 1 flattens them so long shots get real chances. It reshapes odds, it never adds knowledge. OpenAI's API accepts temperature from 0 to 2 (default 1). Anthropic's API accepts 0 to 1 (default 1.0), and its docs now say models released after Claude Opus 4.6 no longer support setting temperature or top_p.

Level 3, top-p. Holtzman et al. (2019) proposed nucleus sampling because a model's distribution has an "unreliable tail" of tens of thousands of low-probability tokens that, added together, get picked too often. Cutting it keeps text coherent without making it repetitive. In their tests nucleus sampling with p = 0.95 scored best on HUSE, a combined human and statistical quality measure (0.97).

Level 4, hallucination. Kalai, Nachum, Vempala and Zhang (OpenAI and Georgia Tech, Sept 2025) argue models hallucinate because training and evaluation reward guessing over admitting uncertainty. Facts with no pattern, like a birthday seen once, can't be learned from patterns, so a well-trained model spreads its bets across plausible answers, and sampling always outputs one of them, fluently. Asked for a birthday three times, DeepSeek-V3 gave three different wrong dates. On accuracy-only tests, a guess beats "I don't know", which always scores zero. OpenAI's SimpleQA comparison: o4-mini 24% right, 75% wrong, 1% abstained; gpt-5-thinking-mini 22% right, 26% wrong, 52% abstained.
