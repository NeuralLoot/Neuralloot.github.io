What's going on

The paper (arXiv 2610.12464; Toyota Research Institute, CMU, UIUC, JHU) trains NVIDIA Cosmos 3 video world models on nested subsets of 300 to 30,000 hours of first-person human manipulation video. It scores the predicted hands and the manipulated objects separately with SCS (mask IoU of tracked hands and objects, prediction vs ground truth) on out-of-distribution clips.

Hands saturate. A saturating fit puts the plain model's hand ceiling at 0.811. A projected skeleton gets there with far less data: at 300 hours it matches what plain training reaches at about 15k hours (~50× less data), and converges to 0.800. Objects stall lower. With the hand pinned by the skeleton, the object curve flattens toward 0.565 and has already reached 93% of that at 30k hours. Against the most the metric can register (0.93 hands, 0.90 objects), hands top out at 86% and objects at 63%.

Bigger model? 4× the parameters (Super, 64B vs Nano, 16B) lifts the object ceiling by just 0.034. In the paper's own arithmetic, closing the remaining 0.205 would take ~6 more quadruplings, about 4 orders of magnitude more parameters, and they call that a lower bound. Their explanation: contact happens under the hand, so the geometry that matters is occluded. Forces and friction never show up in pixels.
