What's real? Paper vs toy parts

The paper. Fluid-Gen-Zero (Hong Huang, Yuqiu Liu, Chenyu You, Daniel Martin, Chuhang Zou, Wuyang Chen; Simon Fraser University, Stony Brook University, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, Meta Reality Labs; arXiv 2610.10984, submitted Oct 7 2026) makes existing video generators respect fluid physics without retraining them. A physics simulator (Genesis, with the liquid as an MPM material) decides how the object and the water move. The frozen video generator still paints the appearance. A vision-language model agent plans where to cut a long video into clips so no cut lands mid-splash.

How the game maps to it. Level 1 shows the kinds of failures the paper targets: objects drifting off their path, water that barely reacts, events in the wrong order. Level 2 is the paper's object rule: the object region is fully anchored to the noised simulation reference early in denoising, then released so the generator can refine how it looks. Level 3 is the water rule: soft guidance that starts strong and decays, so the generator can still add transparency, droplets and texture. The paper notes that a fully clean reference over-constrains appearance and carries proxy-rendering artifacts into the output, which is the "gray sim look" here.

The pushback, from the paper itself. The benchmark is 43 fluid-object samples. The human study had 13 participants (442 pairwise trials), and the low end of same-model preference, 55.1%, is close to a coin flip. On WanMove, aesthetic quality, image quality and the GPT physical-realism score dipped slightly. Guidance is only as good as the scene reconstruction and simulation, and long videos can still drift across clips. Code and data are promised "upon acceptance".
